Volatility measures the wiggle

Volatility is how much a price jumps around. Take the daily log returns ln(St / St−1), find their standard deviation, and scale it up to a year:

σ = √252 × stdev[ ln(St / St−1) ]

A year has about 252 trading days. Independent daily moves add up like a random walk, so the spread grows with the square root of time, not with time itself. A stock with 20% volatility ends a typical year within roughly 20% of where it started, about two years out of three.

That number looks backward. Option prices let us read the market's forecast of volatility looking forward.

Black-Scholes turns volatility into a price

A call option is the right to buy a stock at a fixed strike price K on an expiry date T years away. A put is the right to sell. In 1973 Fischer Black, Myron Scholes and Robert Merton worked out the fair price of a call:

C = S N(d1) − K e−rT N(d2)d1 = ln(S/K) + (r + σ2/2) Tσ √T,  d2 = d1 − σ √T

N(x) is the chance that a standard bell-curve variable is below x. Puts follow from put-call parity, P = C − S + K e−rT. The stock price S, strike, time and interest rate r can all be looked up. The volatility σ cannot, and that missing input is what this whole lab is about.

Where the prices come fromThe lab simulates its market so every answer can be checked. A hidden model called SSVI (Gatheral and Jacquier, 2014) sets a fair volatility for each strike and expiry. We price 13 strikes at each of 9 expiries with Black-Scholes, add random bid-ask jitter, and round to the nearest cent, like a real quote screen. The builder only ever sees those rounded prices. Quotes under 1¢ are thrown out, as a trader would ignore an option with no bid.

The sliders set σ0, σ∞, ρ and η in this hidden model. θ(T) is the at-the-money total variance; it blends short-term and long-term volatility and always grows with T.

Implied volatility: run the formula backwards

We know the market price and want the σ that makes Black-Scholes match it. No algebra can isolate σ here, so we solve it numerically with Newton's method: follow the tangent line of the price curve to where it hits the market price, and repeat.

σn+1 = σn − BS(σn) − Pmarketν(σn),  ν = S φ(d1) √T,  φ(x) = e−x2/2√2π

ν (vega) is the slope of price with respect to volatility. Once Newton's method is close to the answer it roughly doubles the number of correct digits every step. Watch the "miss" column in the lab: the last few steps jump from about 10⁻² to 10⁻⁴ to 10⁻⁸ dollars. Most quotes here finish in 3 to 8 steps, to an accuracy far smaller than a cent.

The starting guess is σ0 = √2 |ln(F/K)| / T (Manaster and Koehler, 1993), where F = S erT is the forward price. That is the inflection point of the price curve: below it the curve bends up, above it the curve bends down, so the tangent steps close in steadily instead of overshooting. For an at-the-money option that guess would be zero, so the solver starts at 10% instead. If a step ever leaves the range known to contain the answer, the solver falls back to bisection, marked bisect in the table.

The lab uses out-of-the-money options only: puts below the forward price, calls above it. Those trade the most, and their prices are almost pure volatility. An in-the-money option's price is mostly its built-in value, so a 1¢ error would swing its implied volatility far more.

Real markets smile

If Black-Scholes were exactly right, every strike and expiry would imply the same σ and the surface would be a flat sheet. It never is. Since the 1987 stock-market crash, traders pay extra for puts that protect against big drops, so low strikes show higher implied volatility. That tilt is the skew; a curve that rises on both sides is a smile.

To describe each expiry's curve with five numbers we use SVI ("stochastic volatility inspired"), a formula Jim Gatheral developed at Merrill Lynch in 1999:

w(k) = a + b ( ρ(k − m) + √(k − m)2 + s2 ),  k = ln(K/F),  w = σ2T

k (log-moneyness) says how far the strike is from the forward price, and w is the total implied variance. Each parameter has one job. (Gatheral writes σ for the last one; we call it s so it is not confused with volatility.)

a
Overall height of the curve
b
How steep the two wings are
ρ
Tilt, between −1 and 1. Negative leans left
m
Slides the curve left or right
s
How rounded the bottom is

Fit each smile to the dots

For each expiry we choose the five parameters that bring the curve as close as possible to the market dots, by minimizing a weighted sum of squared misses:

minimize  Σi νi ( w(ki) − wi )2

The weights νi are vegas, so options whose prices react most to volatility count most. Cheap far-away options, where rounding to the cent is a big relative error, count less.

A trick makes this fast and reliable (Zeliade Systems, 2009). Hold m and s fixed and substitute y = (k − m)/s. The formula becomes

w = a + d y + c √y2 + 1,  c = bs,  d = ρbs

which is linear in a, c and d, so their best values come from solving a 3 × 3 system of linear equations exactly. That leaves a two-dimensional search over m and s, done with the Nelder-Mead method, which tumbles a triangle downhill across the error landscape. Six different starting triangles are tried and the best result is kept. The search keeps m inside the range of quoted strikes and stops s from shrinking to almost zero; otherwise a sharp hockey-stick shape could match flat data perfectly and then shoot off steeply outside it. Finally b = c/s and ρ = d/c.

Stitch the smiles through time

Each expiry now has its own smooth smile. To fill the gaps between expiries we work with total variance w = σ2T at the same log-moneyness, not with volatility itself. Variance is what adds up over time: the variance of a 6-month move is the variance of the first 3 months plus the variance of the next 3.

At every strike we draw a monotone cubic curve through the 9 values w1(k), …, w9(k) (Fritsch and Carlson, 1980). On each gap it is a cubic Hermite polynomial, with t = (T − Ti)/h:

w(k, T) = (2t3 − 3t2 + 1) wi + (t3 − 2t2 + t) h mi + (−2t3 + 3t2) wi+1 + (t3 − t2) h mi+1mi = a + ba/δi−1 + b/δi,  a = 2hi + hi−1,  b = hi + 2hi−1,  σ(k, T) = √w/T

The δ are the straight-line slopes between neighbouring expiries, and each slope mi is their weighted harmonic mean (set to zero when they disagree in sign). That choice has two payoffs. The curve bends smoothly through every expiry, so its time derivative exists everywhere, which the next step needs. And it never overshoots: if total variance rises from one expiry to the next, it rises all the way between them, so the interpolation cannot invent a calendar arbitrage. The lab evaluates this on a grid of 61 strikes × 42 times; the 3D time axis uses a square-root scale so the short expiries are not squashed together.

Local volatility: the stock's own volatility

Implied volatility is one number per option, an average over every path the stock might take to expiry. Bruno Dupire showed in 1994 that the whole surface pins down something sharper: a local volatility σloc(K, T), the volatility the stock would have if it were at price K at time T. A model with exactly this volatility reproduces every option price on the surface. Written with total variance (Gatheral, 2006):

σloc2(k, T) = ∂w / ∂Tg(k),  g(k) = (1 − k w′2w)2 − w′24(1w + 14) + w″2

The top comes from the cubic in time; the bottom needs first and second derivatives across strikes, which the lab takes with central differences, w′ ≈ (w(k+h) − w(k−h))/2h with h = 0.001. Switch the 3D view to Local to see it. Near the money its skew is roughly twice as steep as the implied skew, a classic rule of thumb, because implied volatility averages local volatility along the way from today's price to the strike.

Notice the denominator is the same g(k) as the butterfly test in the next step. Where g ≤ 0 or variance shrinks with time, the square root has no real answer: local volatility is undefined exactly where the surface allows arbitrage. Those cells turn red, and in this lab they almost always sit in the faded corners beyond the last quote, where each expiry's smile is extrapolated on its own.

Check for free money

A surface is only usable if no combination of options priced from it locks in a risk-free profit, called an arbitrage. After every change the builder runs two tests across the strikes that have market quotes. Beyond the outermost quotes the surface is extrapolated, so the tests do not judge it there.

Calendar test

At the same k, total variance must not shrink as expiry grows. Otherwise a longer-dated option could cost less than a matching shorter-dated one.

∂w∂T ≥ 0

Butterfly test

Within each expiry this must stay non-negative. A negative value is a negative probability, so a butterfly spread, which never loses money at expiry, would have a negative price.

g(k) = (1 − k w′2w)2 − w′24(1w + 14) + w″2

Gatheral and Jacquier proved the hidden model has no arbitrage of either kind as long as η(1 + |ρ|) ≤ 2. That is the budget shown under the sliders. Push past it, and step 5 of the readout catches the damage. Heavy noise can also trip it, because each expiry is fitted separately.

Read the market's mind

In 1978 Douglas Breeden and Robert Litzenberger showed that option prices contain a full probability distribution for the future stock price. Differentiate the call price twice with respect to the strike:

q(K) = erT ∂2C∂K2,  Prob(ST < K) = erT ∂P∂K

The "What the market thinks will happen" chart plots this density for the chosen expiry next to the bell curve a flat-volatility world would give. In a stock market the smile puts extra weight on the left: that is the price of crash insurance. These are risk-neutral probabilities, so they mix how likely a drop is with how much traders fear it.

Experiments to run live

Each one resets the lab to a new market and scrolls you back up to watch.

The Black-Scholes world

Skew at zero, the smile almost off, short and long volatility equal. The surface goes nearly flat, which is what Black-Scholes assumes. Real markets never look like this.

A market crash, live

Watch a calm market turn into a crash over a few seconds. Short-dated volatility shoots above long-dated volatility and the term structure flips from sloping up to sloping down.

Messy prices

Noise at 2 volatility points. The dots scatter and the fit error in step 3 climbs, yet the gap to the hidden truth in step 4 stays much smaller, because a smooth curve averages the noise out.

Break the rules

Smile strength and skew past the no-arbitrage budget. The density chart dips below zero, a negative probability, and step 5 flags butterfly arbitrage.

Terms you will hear

Options

Call option
The right, not the obligation, to buy a stock at the strike price on the expiry date.
Put option
The right to sell at the strike price. Works like insurance against a drop.
Strike (K)
The fixed price written into the option. The 3D view shows it as a fraction of today's price.
Expiry (T)
When the option ends, measured in years. 3 months is T = 0.25.
Forward price (F)
Today's price grown at the interest rate, F = S erT. The fair price today for buying the stock at time T.

Volatility

Implied volatility
The volatility that makes Black-Scholes reproduce an option's market price.
Vega (ν)
How much an option's price changes when volatility changes by one unit.
Total variance (w)
Implied volatility squared times time, w = σ²T. It adds up over time, which volatility does not.
Skew and smile
The shape of implied volatility across strikes: tilted (skew) or curved up on both sides (smile).
Arbitrage
A trade that guarantees a profit with no risk. A correct surface must not allow one.
